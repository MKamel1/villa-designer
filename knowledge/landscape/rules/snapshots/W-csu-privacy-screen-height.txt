In Colorado, this generally means protecting the north and west side of the home (Figure 1). Figure 3: Use of an arbor to reduce solar radiation effects. Distance from the home depends on the tree height. The optimum distance for reducing wind velocity is about one to three times tree height. However, a windbreak can provide reasonable protection at a distance of six times the tree’s height. Windbreaks can cause snow drift that can be a nuisance if a driveway is located between the trees and the home. Where possible, extend a row of trees 50 feet beyond the ends of the area being protected. The design and composition of the windbreak depend on the space available and the species and size of trees. Where space is limited, a single row of evergreens is adequate. However, up to five rows of several evergreen species is more effective. Spacing in one-, two- and three-row windbreaks should be 6 feet between trees. Consider the mature shape of the tree when developing a landscape plan for a windbreak. Most windbreaks can also serve other purposes. They provide a visual screen for privacy once they reach 5 to 6 feet high. Well-planned and properly maintained, they are aesthetically pleasing. Choose tree and shrub species that provide protection and food to attract wildlife during the winter. Though it is accurate that evergreens planted close to the home can further reduce the effects of wind, regular drought years have made this an undesirable practice. It is recommended that evergreens be placed in defensible zones 2 and 3 respectively, i.e., 30 to 100 feet, and 100 plus feet away from the home. To avoid fueling any fire further, evergreens should be planted separately in these zones, not in clusters. Evergreens should not be planted under larger trees because they are considered “ladder fuels” due to their oil and resin, or ‘pitch’ content. Deciduous trees and shrubs tend to be more fire resistant. Evergreens planted close to the home can further reduce the effects of wind. If allowed to develop into a thick hedge, spreading evergreens in front of the north and east wall provides additional insulation from the trapped dead airspace they create (Figure 4).
